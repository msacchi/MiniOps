MiniOps.jl

Matrix-free operators for inverse problems, seismic signal processing, and geophysical imaging in Julia

Operators, not matrices

MiniOps.jl is a lightweight Julia framework for building and composing linear operators through their forward and adjoint actions. The central idea is simple: in large-scale inverse problems, we often do not need to form a matrix explicitly. We only need to know how it acts on a vector and how its adjoint acts on data. 

y = A * x
xadj = A' * y

Explicit versus implicit operators

Many linear inverse problems are written in the familiar form

\[ d = A m, \]

where \(m\) is the unknown model, \(d\) is the observed data, and \(A\) is a linear modelling operator. In small problems, \(A\) may be stored explicitly as a matrix. However, in seismic signal processing and geophysical inversion, \(A\) is often far too large to assemble or store.

Instead, the operator is represented implicitly:

\[ m \mapsto A m, \qquad d \mapsto A^{\ast} d. \]

Here \(A^{\ast}\) denotes the adjoint operator. This is the natural language of large-scale inverse problems: modelling and migration, synthesis and analysis, restriction and interpolation, forward wave propagation and adjoint-state backpropagation.

The adjoint test

Every MiniOps operator is expected to satisfy the discrete adjoint identity

\[ \langle A m, d \rangle = \langle m, A^{\ast} d \rangle. \]

This identity is more than a mathematical detail. It is the basic consistency condition required by gradient-based inversion, least-squares migration, sparse recovery, and many iterative algorithms. 

ok, err = adjoint_test(A, m, d)

If this test fails, gradients are wrong, least-squares solvers may behave poorly, and inversion results can be misleading.

Why this matters in seismic processing

Seismic processing is full of operators that are naturally implicit. Examples include:

  • Fourier transforms and inverse Fourier transforms
  • sampling, masking, and trace selection
  • convolution and deconvolution operators
  • Radon transforms
  • blending and deblending operators
  • migration and demigration operators
  • wave-equation modelling and adjoint modelling

For example, a sparse deblending problem may be written as

\[ \min_z \frac{1}{2} \left\| B F z - y \right\|_2^2 + \lambda \|z\|_1, \]

where \(B\) is a blending operator, \(F\) maps Fourier coefficients to data, and \(z\) is sparse in the transform domain. The algorithm does not require the matrix \(BF\). It only requires the actions

\[ z \mapsto BFz, \qquad r \mapsto F^{\ast}B^{\ast}r. \]

MiniOps is designed exactly for this style of computation.

Operator composition

MiniOps operators can be composed algebraically. If

\[ L = A B, \]

then

\[ Lx = A(Bx), \qquad L^{\ast}y = B^{\ast}(A^{\ast}y). \]

In Julia this becomes: 

L = A * B

y = L * x
xadj = L' * y

This allows complex inverse-problem operators to be assembled from simple, testable building blocks.

Example: Radon transform

A frequency-domain linear Radon transform can be written as

\[ \hat d(\omega, x) = \int \hat m(\omega, p) e^{i\omega p x} \, dp. \]

After discretization,

\[ \hat d_n(\omega) = \sum_m \hat m_m(\omega) e^{i\omega p_m x_n}. \]

This looks like a dense matrix multiplication, but it can be implemented without forming the dense matrix. MiniOps supports this style through explicit or chirp-based implementations. 

R = radon_tx_tp_chirp_op(dt, x0, dx, nx, p0, dp, np)

d  = R * m      # tau-p to t-x
ma = R' * d     # t-x to tau-p adjoint

The chirp version uses FFT-based convolution internally, but externally it behaves like any other MiniOps operator.

Example: least-squares inversion

Many geophysical inverse problems have the form

\[ \min_m \frac{1}{2} \| A m - d \|_2^2 + \lambda R(m), \]

where \(R(m)\) is a regularization or sparsity-promoting penalty. The gradient of the data misfit is

\[ \nabla \phi(m) = A^{\ast}(A m - d). \]

Again, this requires only the forward action \(A m\) and the adjoint action \(A^{\ast}r\). No explicit matrix is needed. 

r = A * m - d
g = A' * r

MiniOps philosophy

MiniOps.jl is intentionally small. It is not meant to hide the mathematics. Instead, it exposes the essential computational structure:

  • define a forward operation
  • define its adjoint
  • test the adjoint identity
  • compose operators
  • use them in inversion algorithms

This makes MiniOps useful for teaching, prototyping, and research in seismic signal processing and geophysical inversion.
